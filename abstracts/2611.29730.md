# What can a quantum network promise about the entangled pairs it delivers?

**Authors:** Tanish Singh Rajpal, Michael Mattarock

Quantum networks will give users entangled pairs of qubits, the resource behind teleportation and quantum-secure keys. But a user cannot check a pair without destroying it, so networks promise the quality of each pair, its fidelity, from a physical model rather than from a measurement. If the model is too optimistic, the delivery rule alone may miss the error. We ask: what can a network actually guarantee? Our main result has two sides. By testing some pairs at random, in secret, users can put a lower bound on the average fidelity of the pairs they keep, without any model and at every moment. But when they test only a small fraction and keep most pairs, the tests cannot reliably show that every kept pair meets a fidelity threshold: a rare bad pair can still slip through. A promise about every single pair therefore rests on a model, and we give a rule that bounds each user's risk of ever receiving a bad pair under any data-driven routing, as long as the model is not optimistic or a large enough margin is declared in advance. Real data show calibration noise beyond chance, and in simulated networks a model error of the size seen in one experiment breaks the per-pair promise unless the margin was declared. On real data from trapped-ion and diamond links, the number of pairs decided how strong the certificate was: with about 3,000 pairs of a kilohertz link and 20% tested, the median certificate was 0.818, against the 0.817 we predicted in advance. We then ran the protocol ourselves on a 36-qubit trapped-ion processor in 50 pre-registered jobs. The certificate came within 0.003 of the measured fidelity, dropped by the predicted amount when we secretly damaged a third of the pairs, and, without changes, certified three- and four-qubit entangled states. The same tests also catch an optimistic model, but slowly. We pre-registered every confirmatory analysis and report the failures too.
